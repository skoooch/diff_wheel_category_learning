Debriefing

Thank you for participating in this study in the area of cognitive psychology. The goal of our study is to investigate how learning to sort scene images into categories changes the way people remember and perceive those scenes.

A guiding principle of human cognition research is the use of simple stimuli (e.g., orientation or colour) whose physical properties can be easily manipulated, so researchers can observe corresponding changes in participants' responses. To apply this approach to real-world scenes, we need realistic scene stimuli whose properties can be tightly controlled. In this study we used "scene wheels": circular sets of artificial but highly realistic scene images, generated by diffusion-based image generation models, in which the scenes change continuously and smoothly around the wheel. Some wheels contain a single scene category, while others change from one category to another (e.g., from a bedroom to a kitchen).

In the study, you first learned to assign scene images to two categories using only feedback ("correct" or "wrong"). The boundary between the two categories differed across participants: on one type of wheel it was arbitrary, and on the other it was placed relative to the scene categories already present in the wheel. You then did a memory task in which you briefly saw a scene and, after a short delay, picked the matching scene from the wheel by moving the mouse. By comparing the errors in your memory responses with the category boundary you learned, we will test whether category learning distorts memory and perception of scenes, and whether this depends on how the learned boundary relates to existing scene categories.

Completion of this study will inform our understanding of how categories shape the representation of complex, realistic visual environments. If you are interested in this topic, you may like the following article:

Gaeun Son, Dirk B. Walther, and Michael L. Mack, "Brief category learning distorts perceptual space for complex scenes", Psychonomic Bulletin & Review (2024). https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-024-02484-6
